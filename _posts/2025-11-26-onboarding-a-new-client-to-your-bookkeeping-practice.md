A new client is a business opportunity and a capacity risk at the same time. If onboarding takes three weeks, the practice has spent a third of a month's fee on setup. If onboarding takes two days, the client feels progress immediately and the practice is billing from month one.

The practices that onboard fast all do the same five things.

## One: a standard onboarding checklist

Every new client gets the same list. Not a list tailored to them, not a list built from memory. A standard document that runs from the first meeting to the first monthly report.

The list has four phases:

**Intake, day one.** Client details, business structure, tax obligations, current software and records, bank and M-Pesa details, accountant of record for prior periods.

**Setup, day one to three.** Create the workspace, load the chart of accounts from a template, configure tax settings, invite the client as a viewer, connect or upload opening balances.

**Data collection, day three to ten.** Bank statements for the prior three months (minimum), M-Pesa statements, outstanding invoices, supplier bills, payroll data if there are employees, fixed asset list.

**First month run, day ten to thirty.** Reconcile the opening period, produce the first month's reports, review with the client, agree the reporting cadence going forward.

Every client goes through the same four phases in the same order. The only variation is the volume of data.

## Two: a standard chart of accounts

The single biggest time waster in client onboarding is building a chart of accounts from scratch. It happens when the practice does not have a template, so the accountant constructs the chart based on what they see in the client's books.

That works once. It fails the tenth time, because every client ends up with a chart that is slightly different, which means reports cannot be compared across the practice's book of clients. And when a new staff member joins, they have to learn fifteen variations of the same chart.

A practice needs one chart of accounts, standardised, with the client-specific additions layered on top. The standard chart includes:

- The five account categories in a fixed numbering scheme (1000 to 5999)
- All Kenyan statutory accounts (VAT Input, VAT Output, PAYE Payable, SHIF Payable, NSSF Payable, Housing Levy Payable)
- M-Pesa Float as a dedicated asset
- A bank account slot for each of the client's bank accounts

Additional accounts are added to the client's chart only when the client has a transaction that does not fit the standard chart. Those additions are reviewed before they are made, to prevent the client's chart from drifting into 200 accounts over two years.

## Three: a clear data collection process

The client needs to know exactly what to send, in what format, and by when. This is not a courtesy. It is a capacity decision.

The practices that onboard fast have a one-page document they give every client on day one. It specifies:

- What to send (bank statement, M-Pesa statement, sales invoices, purchase receipts, payroll additions, any other documents)
- What format (PDF, CSV, image files are all fine)
- Where to send it (dedicated email address, shared folder, or client portal)
- By what date (usually the 5th of the following month)
- What happens if it arrives late

Clients who get this on day one send data on time from month one. Clients who are asked for "your records" vaguely send whatever is at hand, four days late.

## Four: a fixed first-month rhythm

The first month is where the practice's value is either established or missed. Do not let it drift.

**Week 1:** setup complete, initial data collected.
**Week 2:** opening balances reconciled, historical records reviewed.
**Week 3:** first month of transactions posted.
**Week 4:** first management report produced and reviewed with the client.

The client should receive a management report in the first month they are a client. Not the second. Not "once we catch up." The first month.

That single fact does more for client retention than any other onboarding decision. A client who receives a P&L and a cash flow in their first month knows what they are paying for. A client who waits two months for a "catch-up" report has already started to wonder if this was a good decision.

## Five: a documented handoff

The day the practice closes on the setup, the client's workspace is complete. Whoever is handling the client after onboarding should be able to open the workspace and see:

- The full chart of accounts
- All historical records loaded
- Bank and M-Pesa reconciled to current
- Tax obligations configured with deadlines
- The client's viewer account active
- Notes on any irregularities from the historical records

If any of those are missing, onboarding is not complete. If they are all in place, the ongoing bookkeeping starts on a clean foundation, and the practice can move the client into the monthly rhythm immediately.

## What onboarding actually costs

A practice doing the above can onboard a new client in 6 to 8 hours of staff time, spread across three weeks. That is roughly one and a half days of billable capacity.

A practice without the checklist, the standard chart, the data collection process, and the fixed first-month rhythm takes 15 to 25 hours. Two to three days of staff time.

At fifteen clients a year, that difference adds up to thirty days of billable capacity, or roughly one full month of a junior accountant. That is the cost of not having a process, and it is invisible in most practices because the time disappears into "setting up a new client" rather than into a line item.
