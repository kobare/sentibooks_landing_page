Every Kenyan business hits the same wall. The employee files are in a drawer. The leave tracker is a spreadsheet that only one person understands. Timesheets arrive in WhatsApp messages and screenshots. Payroll runs in Excel with formulas that were set up three years ago by an accountant who has since left.

That is the point where HR software starts to make sense. Not because the business is big, but because the workarounds have stopped working.

This is a practical guide to what HR software actually does for a Kenyan SME, what to evaluate before buying, and the mistakes that make the investment worthless.

## What HR software is for

HR software solves three problems.

**One: keeping employee data in one place.** Contact details, contracts, statutory identifiers, salary history, leave records, disciplinary notes, training certifications. All searchable, all secure, all in one system rather than scattered across a drawer, a spreadsheet, and someone's email.

**Two: handling the recurring HR work without manual effort.** Payroll, leave approvals, timesheet collection, statutory filings. Each of these has to happen on a schedule. Doing them manually is the source of most HR errors in a small business.

**Three: producing the records that a labour officer or KRA might ask for.** Employment contracts, leave records, payroll records, statutory remittance confirmations. Kenyan law requires these to be kept, and a business that cannot produce them quickly is exposed during any inspection.

Everything else that HR software does, in the full HRMS sense, is optional for an SME.

## What to actually evaluate

The Kenyan HR software market has grown in the last three years. There are now dozens of options, ranging from full HRMS platforms aimed at 200-person companies to focused tools aimed at the 10-person business.

The evaluation criteria that matter for a Kenyan SME, in order of importance:

**Kenyan statutory compliance.** This is the single most important criterion, and the one most providers get wrong. The system must handle:

- PAYE tax bands and personal relief accurately
- SHIF at 2.75 percent of gross, with the KES 300 minimum
- NSSF contributions at both tiers, with the correct lower and upper earnings limits
- Housing Levy at 1.5 percent employee plus 1.5 percent employer
- NITA contributions and annual filing
- P10 annual return generation
- Monthly statutory schedules for NSSF, SHIF, and Housing Levy

If the system gets any of these wrong, it is worse than no software. A miscalculated PAYE is a penalty. A miscalculated NSSF contribution is a liability.

**Casual staff support.** A large share of Kenyan SMEs employ casual staff whose days change weekly. The system should let you mark days worked per casual and calculate their gross pay, statutory deductions, and net pay automatically. If it assumes all employees are salaried, it will not work for a real Kenyan business.

**Leave management with statutory accruals.** 21 working days of annual leave, maternity at three months, paternity at two weeks. These should be built in, not configured by you. The system should also handle sick leave, compassionate leave, and unpaid leave, with the accrual and balance calculations tracked automatically.

**Timesheet collection.** If the business employs casuals or contract workers, timesheet collection is essential. Ideally the system should let staff submit their own timesheets from a phone, with supervisor approval.

**Employee records with statutory identifiers.** KRA PIN, NSSF number, SHIF number, NITA registration, employment contract dates, salary history. All searchable, all attached to the employee record.

**Payroll connected to accounting.** When payroll runs, the salary expense, the statutory liabilities, and the net payment should flow directly into the accounting books. If the business has to manually re-enter payroll into the accounting system, that is a source of errors.

**Pricing that matches the business size.** Most Kenyan HR software is priced per employee per month. For a 20-employee business, that should mean KES 3,000 to KES 8,000 a month. If the pricing is significantly above that, the business is paying for modules it will not use.

## The common mistakes

Four mistakes turn the HR software investment into a waste of money.

**Buying a full HRMS for a small team.** A 15-employee business does not need performance reviews, recruitment tracking, or engagement surveys. Those modules cost money and take time to implement, and they will not be used. Buy the software that does what you actually do.

**Skipping the payroll test.** Before buying, ask the vendor to run one month of your actual payroll through the system in front of you. Not a demo payroll, your real payroll. If the net pay does not match what you calculate manually, the system is not ready.

**Underestimating implementation time.** Even a focused HR system takes two to four weeks to set up properly: entering employee records, configuring statutory settings, testing payroll, training the person who will run it. If the vendor promises same-day setup, they are overselling. Plan for the time.

**Not training the person who will actually use it.** Most HR software failures are adoption failures. The system is good, but the person who runs payroll never learned to use it properly, so they fall back to the spreadsheet. The right training programme is two sessions: one at setup, one a month later once they have used it in anger.

## What HR software is worth

The financial case for a Kenyan SME:

**Time saved on payroll.** From a day per month to an hour. 11 days a year of the accountant's time recovered.

**Penalties avoided.** A single miscalculated PAYE or missed NSSF remittance penalty is KES 5,000 to KES 20,000. Over a year, most small businesses that run payroll manually trigger at least one.

**Leave disputes avoided.** Manual leave tracking loses 3 to 5 days per employee per year to accrual errors. For a 20-employee business at KES 2,000 per day cost, that is KES 120,000 to KES 200,000 per year in overpaid leave or disputes.

**Casual staff accuracy.** Casual pay calculated manually is wrong roughly 8 percent of the time in our experience. At KES 500 per day error across 20 casuals working 20 days a month, that is KES 200,000 a year.

The cumulative value for a 20-employee Kenyan SME is KES 250,000 to KES 400,000 a year, against a software cost of KES 36,000 to KES 96,000 a year. The ROI is typically under three months.
