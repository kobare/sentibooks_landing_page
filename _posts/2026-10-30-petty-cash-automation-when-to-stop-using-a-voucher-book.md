The voucher book works. That is the problem. It works long past the point where it should, and by the time it stops working, the business has already absorbed a year of invisible cost.

This is the honest guide to knowing when manual petty cash stops being an asset and starts being a liability.

## What the voucher book actually costs

A voucher book itself is cheap. A few hundred shillings, a lockable box, and a custodian who keeps the entries in order. For a business with a handful of small expenses a week, it is entirely adequate.

What is not cheap is the time it takes to keep it clean. Each expense requires:

- A voucher filled in by hand
- The receipt attached
- An entry in the petty cash book
- Approval from someone with authority
- A reconciliation at month end

At five expenses a week, that is roughly 90 minutes of total admin time per month. At 20 expenses a week, it is close to six hours. At 40, it is a full working day.

The first inflection point is not about cost. It is about where that admin time is going. When the accountant is spending a full day a month on petty cash, the business is paying an accountant's salary to do work a spreadsheet could do in a third of the time.

## The three thresholds

Three separate thresholds matter. Most businesses cross them in order, and each one changes what the right system is.

### Threshold one: 15 to 20 expenses a week

This is where the voucher book starts to slip. Not dramatically. One receipt goes missing. One voucher does not get the second signature. One month end reconciliation comes out KES 400 short and nobody knows why.

At this volume, the process still technically works, but the controls start to bend. And the bending is where the losses begin.

The fix at this stage is not automation. It is better process. Weekly reconciliation rather than monthly. Two signatures required instead of one. A monthly spot check on a random 20 percent of vouchers.

### Threshold two: 40 to 60 expenses a week

At this volume, weekly reconciliation takes an hour. The custodian is spending a third of their time on petty cash. Expenses slip through the crack between voucher creation and cash disbursement.

At this point, the volume justifies a change. The specific change depends on how much of the petty cash is genuinely small and local versus how much is standing in for what should be a proper purchase order.

Three options open up at this threshold:

**Switch to a dedicated expense card.** Give the custodian a card with a small limit, and every expense flows through the card statement. This works if the bank or fintech provider the business already uses offers it. It eliminates the cash handling entirely, which removes about 60 percent of the admin.

**Use a simple expense app.** A mobile app that takes a photo of the receipt, records the amount, and pushes it into the books. This is the cheap version and it works well if the custodian is comfortable with a phone. The trade-off is that the voucher book becomes a backup, not a primary record, and the business has to decide which is authoritative.

**Move petty cash into the accounting system.** Every expense is logged against a job, a client, or a cost centre, and the petty cash account balance updates in real time. This is the fullest version and it costs the most to set up. It also makes the month-end close trivially easy because the books are already up to date.

### Threshold three: 100 or more expenses a week

At this volume, petty cash is no longer really petty. The business is moving large amounts of cash through a system designed for small amounts, and the controls are not keeping up.

At 100 expenses a week, the realistic answer is that most of those expenses are not petty cash. They are operating expenses that should be on a purchase order, a supplier invoice, or a credit card with a proper statement. The business is using petty cash as a workaround for not having a purchasing process.

The right move at this threshold is not better petty cash management. It is restructuring the expenses themselves so most of them leave the petty cash flow.

## The automation decision

If the volume has reached the point where the voucher book is a full day of work per month, the business is ready for some level of automation.

There are three levels, in order of cost and complexity.

**Level one: digital expense capture.** A phone app that photographs receipts and records amounts. No cash handling change. About 20 minutes to set up and 2 minutes per expense to use. Best for businesses at the 15 to 30 expense threshold.

**Level two: digital petty cash float.** A digital ledger that tracks the float, replaces the petty cash book, and keeps the reconciliation current. The physical cash still exists, but its movements are recorded in real time. About two hours to set up and roughly 30 seconds per expense. Best for businesses at the 40 to 100 expense threshold.

**Level three: eliminate the float.** Move to a dedicated expense card or a reimbursement model, so there is no physical petty cash box. This is a process change as much as a software change. The right fit is usually above 100 expenses a week or when the business is running multiple branches.

## What actually saves the money

The saving from automation is not the software cost. It is the time recovered and the leakage stopped.

A business running at 40 expenses a week that moves to digital petty cash capture typically recovers 4 to 5 hours a month of admin time. That is roughly half a day of an admin or accountant's salary, every month.

The leakage side is harder to quantify but larger. Cash-based systems lose 3 to 8 percent of the amount moved, on average. Some of that is error. Some is convenience. A small percentage is outright theft. Digital capture brings that down to 1 to 2 percent because every expense has a timestamp, a photo, and a person attached.

For a business moving KES 100,000 a month through petty cash, the difference between 5 percent leakage and 1.5 percent leakage is KES 42,000 a year recovered, before any time savings.

## The one habit that matters more than the tool

Whatever system the business uses, the single most important habit is daily capture. Expenses recorded the day they happen have a 90 percent chance of staying accurate. Expenses batched into a weekly or monthly session have a 40 percent chance of a material error somewhere in the pile.

Daily capture also changes the cultural expectation. When the custodian knows that every expense gets logged before the end of the day, the habit of "I'll do it later" never forms. That alone stops most leakage.

## Getting started

If the business is at 15 to 30 expenses a week and the voucher book still technically works, start with the lightest possible fix. A phone app or a simple spreadsheet that lives on the custodian's phone and gets shared with the accountant weekly.

If the business is at 40 to 100 expenses a week, move to a proper digital petty cash ledger. The transition takes about a week of training and pays for itself within three months.

Above 100, the right answer is usually to restructure the expenses rather than manage them better. That conversation is worth having before investing in any new system.
